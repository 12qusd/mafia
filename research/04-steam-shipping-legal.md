# Steam Shipping & Legal Analysis

## Shipping on Steam — steps

1. Create a Steamworks partner account at partner.steamgames.com/steamdirect: requires a Steam account that has spent at least $5, legal entity/individual info, US tax interview (W-8BEN for non-US individuals -- relevant since user appears Japan-based), banking details for payouts, and identity verification. Onboarding paperwork typically clears in 1-3 days. (Source: https://partner.steamgames.com/steamdirect)
2. Pay the Steam Direct fee: $100 USD per app, non-refundable but recoupable once the app reaches $1,000 Adjusted Gross Revenue (store + in-app). (Source: https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/appfee)
3. Hard timing constraints baked into Steam Direct: you cannot release until 30 days after paying the app fee, AND your 'Coming Soon' store page must be publicly visible for at least 2 weeks before launch. Plan ~30 days minimum from signup to earliest possible release. (Sources: https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/appfee, https://ziva.sh/blogs/publish-game-steam)
4. Build the store page: name, description, capsule art in all required sizes, at least 5 screenshots, trailer, tags, pricing, content survey. Submit the store page for Valve review -- typically 3-5 business days; rejections (usually for capsule art or missing assets) restart the cycle. Once approved, set it live as 'Coming Soon' to start the 2-week clock and accumulate wishlists. (Sources: https://partner.steamgames.com/steamdirect, https://www.summerengine.com/blog/how-to-publish-game-on-steam)
5. Upload builds via SteamPipe: use the SDK's ContentBuilder (steamcmd + run_app_build), with an app_build_[AppID].vdf referencing depot_build_[DepotID].vdf scripts that map local files into depots. Files are chunked (~1MB), diffed, compressed, encrypted, and uploaded; each upload gets a BuildID you assign to a branch. (Source: https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/sdk/uploading)
6. Depot layout for Windows+macOS: create one depot per platform, set each depot's OS filter (Windows-only / macOS-only), keep shared assets in an all-OS depot if desired, and define separate launch options with 'oslist' (windows / macos) pointing at the right executable (.exe vs .app bundle). (Source: https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/sdk/uploading)
7. Use beta branches for testing: push builds to a passworded beta branch, test via the real Steam client (overlay, invites, auth all behave differently than dev builds), then promote the build to the default branch. Setting the default branch live requires phone/Steam Mobile two-factor on the uploading account. (Source: https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/sdk/uploading)
8. Submit the build for release review: Valve runs the game to check it launches, matches the store page, and follows content guidelines. Typically 1-5 business days. Multiplayer-only games should provide Valve test instructions/accounts since reviewers must be able to reach gameplay. (Sources: https://ziva.sh/blogs/publish-game-steam, https://pixune.com/blog/how-to-publish-a-game-on-steam/)
9. Steam Deck: Valve automatically runs a Deck compatibility review post-launch and assigns Verified/Playable/Unsupported. There is no macOS path on Deck -- it runs the WINDOWS depot under Proton on Linux, so the Windows build is what gets tested. For a chat-heavy social deduction game the make-or-break items are: text input (invoke the on-screen keyboard via ISteamUtils::ShowFloatingGamepadTextInput or you fail review), full controller support or touch fallback, legible UI/fonts at 1280x800, and any anti-cheat/networking must work under Proton. You can also request a re-review after fixes. (Sources: https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/steamhardware/compat, https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/steamdeck/proton)
10. Realistic total timeline 2026: ~1 week onboarding + store page build, 3-5 days store review, 2+ weeks Coming Soon, 1-5 days build review, gated by the 30-day post-fee minimum -- so 4-6 weeks if everything is ready. (Source: https://ziva.sh/blogs/publish-game-steam, https://www.summerengine.com/blog/how-to-publish-game-on-steam)

## Relevant Steamworks features

### Lobbies & Matchmaking (ISteamMatchmaking)

Core fit for a Mafia-style game. Steam lobbies are exactly the 'named room people join before the game starts' primitive: create lobby, set arbitrary key/value lobby metadata (room name, player count, game mode/role list), search/filter the lobby list, lobby chat before start. Important caveat from Valve's own docs: the lobby is matchmaking only -- 'the lobby itself does not facilitate multiplayer gameplay. That's your job, through your networking layer.' Free, no servers needed for the lobby phase. (Source: https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/multiplayer/matchmaking)

### Friend invites & Rich Presence (ISteamFriends)

High value, near-free virality: ISteamMatchmaking::InviteUserToLobby sends a Steam chat invite with a one-click join link; Rich Presence 'status' key shows 'In lobby 7/15 - Night 3' in the friends list and the 'connect' key enables the Join Game button on a friend's profile. Up to 20 RP keys per user, localizable. For a party game whose growth loop is 'friend sees friend playing', this is one of the highest-ROI integrations. (Sources: https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/multiplayer/matchmaking, https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/api/isteamfriends)

### Steam Auth (ISteamUser session tickets)

Eliminates running your own account system entirely. Client calls ISteamUser::GetAuthTicketForWebApi('your-backend-identity-string'), sends the ticket to your backend, backend calls the ISteamUserAuth/AuthenticateUserTicket Web API with the same identity string, and gets back a verified 64-bit SteamID plus ownership/ban flags. SteamID becomes the durable player identity for stats, friends, bans, and progression -- no passwords, no email verification, no GDPR-laden credential store. Best practice: one identity string per consuming service. (Sources: https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/auth, https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/webapi/isteamuserauth)

### Networking: ISteamNetworkingSockets / ISteamNetworkingMessages + Steam Datagram Relay (SDR)

Two viable topologies for a 15-player social deduction game: (a) host-as-server P2P -- CreateListenSocketP2P/ConnectP2P automatically routes through Valve's SDR relay backbone, which hides all player IPs (critical for a game where angry players might dox/DDoS the lynch-vote winner) and often improves ping; (b) your own authoritative dedicated servers -- arguably better for Mafia since the host must not be able to read hidden roles; dedicated-server SDR access requires applying to Valve, or you can just use plain TCP/WebSockets to your own infra and use Steam only for auth/lobbies. ISteamNetworkingMessages offers a connectionless UDP-like API if preferred. (Sources: https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/multiplayer/networking, https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/multiplayer/steamdatagramrelay, https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/api/ISteamnetworkingSockets)

### Achievements & Stats (ISteamUserStats)

Standard integration ('win as Jester', 'survive 10 games'); configured in the Steamworks partner site, low effort, meaningfully improves store page appeal and retention. Note for trust systems: stats are client-writable unless you push them server-side via the Web API, so don't hang anti-abuse logic off client-reported stats.

### Anti-abuse: Game Bans via ICheatReportingService -- NOT VAC

VAC is irrelevant here: it detects client-side cheat software (wallhacks, injectors) on VAC-secured servers; the abuse vector in Mafia is behavioral -- gamethrowing, chat abuse, vote-griefing, multi-accounting. What applies instead: (1) Game Bans -- developer-issued bans through the ICheatReportingService Web API (publisher key, secure-server-only); banned players get a permanent 'Game Ban' marker on their Steam profile and are blocked from secure multiplayer for the duration you set, which is the real deterrent because ban evasion costs a new game purchase; (2) your own report/review pipeline keyed on verified SteamIDs from Steam Auth (authoritative server logs chat + actions, players file reports, you or moderators issue bans); (3) ISteamUtils::FilterText -- Steam's built-in legally-required text filtering for user chat, very relevant for a chat-centric game; (4) account-quality signals (Steam account age, phone-verified, prior game bans visible via Web API) to gate ranked play. (Sources: https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/anticheat, https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/webapi/ICheatReportingService, https://help.steampowered.com/en/faqs/view/46DB-4CEC-F7E9-49E5)

## macOS notes

Valve policy since October 2019 ('Updated Requirements for New macOS Applications on Steam', https://steamcommunity.com/groups/steamworks/eventcomments/1742266164814599752/): new macOS apps on Steam must be 64-bit and must be signed + notarized with an Apple Developer ID. Practical chain: (1) join the Apple Developer Program ($99/yr) and obtain a 'Developer ID Application' certificate; (2) sign every executable, dylib, and framework in the .app bundle inside-out with codesign, using --timestamp and the hardened runtime (hardened runtime is mandatory for notarization); (3) CRITICAL Steam-specific gotcha: enable the com.apple.security.cs.disable-library-validation entitlement -- without Disable Library Validation the Steam overlay/Steamworks dylib injection fails and 'the app will not work right when launched from Steam' (https://blog.xojo.com/2026/03/24/code-signing-on-macos-what-developers-need-to-know-part-3/); (4) submit with notarytool (altool is dead), wait for the ticket, then staple it (stapler staple) so Gatekeeper can verify offline (https://developer.apple.com/documentation/security/notarizing-macos-software-before-distribution, https://developer.apple.com/developer-id/). Nuances: files downloaded by the Steam client don't carry the quarantine xattr, so Gatekeeper often wouldn't block an unsigned app in practice -- but Valve requires notarization for new apps regardless, and signing protects users who copy the app around. Apple Silicon: ship a universal (arm64 + x86_64) binary or accept Rosetta 2 translation; for a lightweight lobby game either works, but native arm64 is increasingly expected in 2026. Architect the build pipeline so the .app is signed/notarized BEFORE the SteamPipe depot upload (SteamPipe must not modify the bundle afterward or the signature breaks -- upload the exact notarized bundle). macOS depot gets its own launch option with oslist=macos pointing at the .app. Also note Steam Deck does NOT use the macOS build -- it runs the Windows depot under Proton -- so macOS support buys you Mac players only.

## Legal analysis

> Practical risk assessment, not legal advice.

FRAMEWORK (not legal advice -- practical risk assessment). Three legal layers apply to cloning SC2Mafia: (1) copyright in the SC2Mafia map's expression, complicated by Blizzard's ownership claim; (2) the non-copyrightability of game mechanics; (3) trademark around names. --- LAYER 1, Blizzard's claim: Blizzard's Custom Game Acceptable Use Policy (https://www.blizzard.com/en-us/legal/2749df07-2b53-4990-b75e-a7cb3610318b/custom-game-acceptable-use-policy) states 'Custom Games are and shall remain the sole and exclusive property of Blizzard' and the creator 'hereby assign[s] to Blizzard all of your rights, title, and interest' including copyrights, and bars selling custom games. Consequence: whatever copyrightable expression exists IN the SC2Mafia map (Galaxy trigger code, in-map text, role description strings, any custom art, the map file itself) is claimed by Blizzard, and Dark.Revenant likely couldn't license it to you even if willing. So the clone must not lift anything FROM the map: no exported assets, no decompiled triggers, no verbatim text. A clean-room reimplementation of the RULES in your own engine with your own assets/text is outside the AUP's reach -- the AUP governs content made WITH Blizzard's tools, and rules/mechanics aren't ownable by anyone (see Layer 2). Enforcement reality check: the Valve-Blizzard DotA dispute (settled May 2012) shows Blizzard's actual posture -- the fight was over the 'DotA' TRADEMARK, not over copyright in mechanics; Valve kept commercial rights to the Dota name and shipped Dota 2, built openly on a WC3 custom map's design (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/blizzard-and-valve-settle-ongoing-dota-trademark-controversy, https://liquipedia.net/dota2/Blizzard_Entertainment,_Inc._v_Valve_Corporation). Blizzard has never, as far as the record shows, sued a standalone remake of a custom map's mechanics that used original assets and a non-Blizzard name. --- LAYER 2, mechanics are not copyrightable (idea/expression distinction, 17 U.S.C. 102(b)): Tetris Holding v. Xio Interactive, 863 F.Supp.2d 394 (D.N.J. 2012) -- rules and functional gameplay are unprotectable ideas, BUT the specific audiovisual expression ('look and feel': piece colors/shapes/dimensions, board proportions, visual style) is protected; Xio lost because it copied the visuals nearly pixel-for-pixel, not because it copied falling-block rules (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetris_Holding,_LLC_v._Xio_Interactive,_Inc.). DaVinci Editrice v. ZiKo Games (S.D. Tex. 2014) -- a functionally identical clone of the card game Bang! with different art/theme did NOT infringe: 'game mechanics and the rules are not entitled to protection,' including the roles-and-interactions structure (Sheriff/Deputy/Outlaw/Renegade -- directly analogous to Town/Mafia/Neutral role structures) (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/texas-court-affirms-game-mechanics-not-protected-under-copyright-law, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-txsd-4_13-cv-03415/pdf/USCOURTS-txsd-4_13-cv-03415-0.pdf). Spry Fox v. LOLApps (W.D. Wash. 2012) -- the cautionary middle case: court excluded functional elements (6x6 grid) and scenes-a-faire (coins as score) but held the overall selection/arrangement of expressive elements plus closely-mapped theme (bear->yeti, bush->sapling) could state a claim; aggravated by the defendant having had NDA access. Lesson: don't do a 1:1 reskin where every expressive element has an obvious counterpart, and don't copy the 'total concept and feel' (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spry_Fox,_LLC_v._Lolapps,_Inc.). --- LAYER 3, the underlying game is public domain: Mafia/Werewolf was created by Dimitry Davidoff at Moscow State University in 1986 and has been freely reimplemented for decades (Werewolf variant by Andrew Plotkin 1997); nobody owns day/night, lynching, or informed-minority-vs-uninformed-majority (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mafia_(party_game)). --- THE KEY PRECEDENT, Town of Salem: BlankMediaGames' Town of Salem (Steam, Dec 2014) is openly and admittedly based on SC2Mafia -- community sources confirm 'Town of Salem is based off of SC2:Mafia' and SC2Mafia's creator Dark.Revenant even helped promote it (http://www.sc2mafia.com/forum/archive/index.php/t-25658.html, https://steamcommunity.com/app/334230/discussions/0/610573009238451786/). ToS kept the day/night/trial structure, the faction layout, the role MECHANICS, and even dozens of literal ROLE NAMES from SC2Mafia (Jailor, Veteran, Escort, Consort, Lookout, Spy, Blackmailer, Framer, Janitor, Disguiser, Consigliere, Mafioso, Godfather, Bodyguard, Investigator, Witch, Arsonist, Serial Killer, Jester, Executioner, Amnesiac, Survivor...) -- it changed the name, the theme (Salem witch trials), and all art/audio/text, and it shipped commercially for a decade with zero Blizzard action. A second-generation precedent exists too: Throne of Lies and other ToS-likes shipped fine on Steam. --- ROLE NAMES analysis: copyright does not protect names, titles, or short phrases at all (US Copyright Office Circular 33), so role names are only ever a trademark/trade-dress question, and neither Blizzard nor BMG has trademarked individual role names. Generic tier (predates SC2Mafia in the broader Mafia community -- MafiaScum/EpicMafia used Cop/Sheriff, Doctor, Vigilante, Godfather, Mayor, Jester, Serial Killer, Survivor, Mason long before 2010): zero risk. Middle tier (popularized by SC2Mafia, copied wholesale by Town of Salem: Jailor, Escort/Consort, Veteran, Lookout, Consigliere, Framer, Janitor, Blackmailer, Disguiser, Witch, Arsonist, Executioner, Amnesiac): very low risk -- ToS itself proves these traveled freely, and as single descriptive words they're unprotectable; copying the ENTIRE list verbatim with identical descriptions edges toward a selection-and-arrangement claim (Spry Fox), so vary the lineup and write all descriptions fresh. Distinctive tier (SC2Mafia-specific flavor, esp. the Triad faction's Chinese-themed names -- Dragon Head, Incense Master, Vanguard, Enforcer, Administrator, Silencer, Informant, Liaison, Sweeper -- plus oddballs like Beguiler/Auditor): these are SC2Mafia's recognizable creative fingerprint; individually still just short phrases, but reproducing the whole distinctive set signals copying and invites a look-and-feel argument -- rename or re-theme these. --- BOTTOM LINE RISK: a clean-room clone with original name/art/text/theme that reuses the mechanics and the generic-to-middle-tier role vocabulary sits squarely in DaVinci v. ZiKo safe territory, is exactly what Town of Salem already did commercially without incident, and the realistic Blizzard risk is approximately zero provided no StarCraft marks/assets appear anywhere. The marginally larger practical risk is actually a look-and-feel complaint from BlankMediaGames if the clone reads as a Town-of-Salem reskin -- so differentiate theme/UI from ToS as well as from SC2.

## MUST change (be original)

- The name 'SC2Mafia' -- 'SC2' invokes Blizzard's StarCraft trademark; pick a wholly original title (also check USPTO/Steam for collisions, and note 'Mafia' alone collides with 2K's Mafia franchise trademark for video games -- safer as part of a longer distinctive mark or avoided)
- Any StarCraft II assets: unit models/portraits used as character avatars, Blizzard sound effects, music, UI textures, fonts, icons -- all Blizzard property, zero tolerance
- Any code or data exported from the map: Galaxy script, trigger logic, the map file, data tables -- Blizzard claims ownership of the map's contents under the Custom Game AUP; reimplement from observed behavior (clean-room), never from extracted files
- All verbatim text from SC2Mafia: role descriptions, help/tutorial text, flavor text, investigator result strings, death announcements -- rewrite everything from scratch even where the underlying fact conveyed is the same
- References to Blizzard, Battle.net, StarCraft, or Arcade anywhere in the game, store page, or marketing; do not market it as 'SC2Mafia on Steam' (nominative references in press interviews about inspiration are fine; trading on the mark in the product is not)
- The SC2Mafia logo and any community-made art associated with the map
- The distinctive SC2Mafia-specific role-name set, especially the Triad faction's themed names (Dragon Head, Incense Master, Vanguard, Enforcer, Administrator, Silencer, Informant, Liaison, Sweeper) and oddball original names (e.g. Beguiler, Auditor) -- rename or re-theme; these are the map's creative fingerprint
- Avoid a 1:1 expressive mapping (Spry Fox lesson): don't reproduce the exact full role list, exact setup lists, and exact UI layout simultaneously -- vary the lineup, add original roles, design an original visual theme distinct from BOTH StarCraft and Town of Salem's Salem-witch-trial trade dress
- Original visual identity overall: character art, setting/theme, UI design, color language, sound -- the 'total look and feel' is where Tetris v. Xio says clones actually lose

## Safe to keep (mechanics)

- The core Mafia/Werewolf framework itself: hidden roles, informed minority vs uninformed majority, day/night cycle -- public domain party game (Davidoff 1986), unprotectable rules
- Day/night phase structure with timed phases, night actions resolved simultaneously, day discussion
- Voting, trial, defense statement, and lynch mechanics (vote to put on trial, defense speech, guilty/innocent vote) -- pure mechanics under DaVinci v. ZiKo / 17 USC 102(b)
- Faction structure: Town vs Mafia vs a second evil faction vs Neutrals, with faction win conditions -- role-interaction structure explicitly held unprotectable in DaVinci v. ZiKo
- Role MECHANICS as behaviors: investigate alignment, heal/protect, roleblock, jail-and-execute, douse-then-ignite, witch-control, bus-drive/swap, frame, clean death, disguise, last-will notes, whispers, death notes -- mechanics are ideas, not expression
- Generic role names with pre-SC2Mafia community provenance: Sheriff, Doctor, Investigator/Detective, Vigilante, Mayor, Bodyguard, Godfather, Mafioso, Jester, Serial Killer, Survivor, Witch, Mason -- standard Mafia-genre vocabulary (scenes-a-faire), used across MafiaScum/EpicMafia/Town of Salem/Town of Salem 2 without incident
- Middle-tier role names popularized by SC2Mafia and already freely propagated through Town of Salem (Jailor, Veteran, Escort, Lookout, Consigliere, Framer, Janitor, Blackmailer, Disguiser, Arsonist, Executioner, Amnesiac) -- individually safe as unprotectable short phrases; just don't clone the entire roster verbatim with identical descriptions
- Lobby/host model: 15-player named lobbies, host-configurable role lists, repick/host migration -- functional design, fully reimplementable
- Game-flow conventions: graveyard with revealed roles, last words, whisper system, day chat / mafia night chat split -- functional/scenes-a-faire for the genre
- Marketing truthfully in interviews/devlogs that the game is 'inspired by the classic Mafia party game' and by the SC2 Arcade community scene -- factual statements, nominative fair use; just keep marks out of the title/store assets
